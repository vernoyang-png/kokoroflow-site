Before you reset or repair: a data-safety checklist

A calm, data-first checklist for spotting stop-now warning signs, protecting your files, and handing a computer to a repair shop without unnecessary risk.

Reviewed: November 2026 · Written by a computer technician with nearly 30 years of repair experience

Checklist 0%
Three rules before you touch anything:
  • Do not reset, reinstall or “clean” the computer yet.
  • Do not keep restarting a device that sounds or smells wrong.
  • Protect irreplaceable data before trying to improve performance.
Your computer is replaceable. Your only copy of a photo, document or recovery key may not be.
Smoke, sparks, a swollen battery, or liquid near power? Stop using the device. If it is safe, disconnect power without touching a hot, swollen, wet or damaged area. Move away from fire risk and contact a qualified repair or emergency professional. Do not charge a swollen battery.

Step 1: Check for stop-now warning signs

Tick anything you notice. One serious warning is enough to stop experimenting.

Stop using the computer. Do not run scans, updates, repair tools or a large backup. Power it down if safe and contact a reputable repair or data-recovery professional. Tell them exactly what you observed.
Use caution. Keep use brief, avoid updates and stress tests, and protect essential files first if the computer stays stable.

Step 2: Is it safe to attempt a backup?

Only try a normal backup when there are no stop-now signs and the computer can stay stable long enough to copy essential files.

Important: cloud sync is not automatically a complete backup. Deleted, encrypted or corrupted files may sync too. Confirm that important files open correctly from another device or backup location.

Step 3: Protect the data in priority order

1 Irreplaceable personal files

Photos, videos, current work, family records, creative projects, tax and legal documents.

2 Access and recovery

Password-manager access, two-factor recovery codes, BitLocker recovery key, software licenses.

3 Local app data

Email archives, accounting files, browser bookmarks, templates, game saves, specialized databases.

4 Replaceable material

Installers, cached media, and apps or files you can download again.

Step 4: Prepare a safer repair handoff

A technician may need access to test the problem, but that does not always mean access to every personal account.

Step 5: Repair, upgrade, replace, or stop?

Repair

The failure is isolated, parts are available, the cost is reasonable, and the PC still meets your security and performance needs.

Upgrade

The PC is reliable and supported, but storage or memory is the clear bottleneck. Confirm compatibility first. See is an SSD upgrade worth it?

Replace

Several parts are aging, security support is ending, repair approaches replacement cost, or reliability now matters more than another year of use.

Stop and recover data

The drive is mechanically failing, the device is unsafe, or the data is worth more than the machine. Avoid repeated DIY attempts.

Windows 10 note: Microsoft ended standard support for Windows 10 on October 14, 2025. An otherwise usable PC may have a limited option through Extended Security Updates (ESU), but that does not fix failing hardware. Compare all choices in Windows 10 PC can't upgrade? 5 safe options.

Frequently asked questions

Should I reset Windows to fix a slow or unstable PC?

Not before your important files are safely copied somewhere else and you have ruled out warning signs such as clicking, read errors or a disappearing drive. A reset can make data recovery harder.

Is cloud sync the same as a backup?

Not always. Deleted, encrypted or corrupted files can sync too. Confirm that important files open correctly from another device or a separate backup.

What should I tell a repair shop about my data?

Put it in writing: do not reset, format, reinstall, erase or replace the drive without contacting you first, and ask for the original drive and replaced parts back.
