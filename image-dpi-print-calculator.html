DPI vs pixels

Changing a DPI label alone does not create detail. Print quality depends mainly on pixel dimensions divided by print size. This calculator uses pixels per inch, commonly called DPI in print workflows.

Quick guide

  • 300 PPI: high-quality close viewing
  • 150 PPI: posters and larger prints
  • 72 PPI: screen reference, usually low for print

What problem does this DPI calculator solve?

It converts pixel dimensions into an estimated physical print size at a chosen PPI/DPI. It helps you check whether an image contains enough pixels for a photo, poster, card, or other printed item.

How to calculate print size

  1. Choose an image to read its dimensions locally, or enter width and height in pixels.
  2. Enter the target PPI, such as 300 for close-viewed printing.
  3. Select inches or centimeters.
  4. Review the calculated print width and height.
  5. Confirm final requirements with your printer.

Frequently asked questions

Are DPI and PPI the same?

PPI describes image pixels per inch; DPI technically describes printer dots. They are often used interchangeably in print instructions, but your printer may distinguish them.

Can I increase quality by changing the DPI label?

No. Print detail comes primarily from actual pixel dimensions. Relabelling DPI alone does not add pixels.

Is 300 PPI always required?

No. Viewing distance, print method, paper, subject, and printer requirements affect the suitable resolution.
