Freelance planning guide

Set a freelance rate from capacity, not wishful hours

A sustainable starting rate covers personal income, business costs, non-billable time, and uncertainty.

Reviewed: October 2026 · 6 minute read

Start with the revenue requirement

Add desired personal income and annual business costs. Business costs can include software, equipment, insurance, accounting, training, marketing, workspace, platform fees, and payment charges. Then add a contingency or profit margin for slower periods and reinvestment.

Estimate billable capacity honestly

A 40-hour working week rarely contains 40 billable hours. Proposals, administration, lead generation, client communication, bookkeeping, and professional development still consume time. Multiply realistic billable hours by realistic working weeks—not by every hour in the calendar.

Baseline rate = annual revenue requirement ÷ annual billable hours.

Hourly rate versus project price

The calculated hourly rate is a floor for planning. A project price should also reflect scope, complexity, urgency, responsibility, revisions, meetings, usage rights, and the value of the outcome. Estimate the work internally, add risk, and describe deliverables clearly.

Example

A freelancer wants 60,000 of personal income, expects 12,000 of business costs, adds a 15% margin, and can bill 25 hours across 46 weeks. The revenue target is 82,800 and billable capacity is 1,150 hours, producing a baseline near 72 per hour before country-specific tax considerations.

Review the rate regularly

Compare estimated hours with actual hours, including revisions and communication. Track win rate, late payments, utilization, and costs. Raise or restructure rates when repeated projects require more effort than the estimate supports.

Important limitations

This calculation does not determine tax, legal status, minimum rates, or market demand. Rules vary by location and profession. Use an accountant or adviser for personal tax and business-structure decisions.

In depth

Going further: checking your rate against reality

A cost-based rate gives you a floor. Before quoting it, check it from two other directions: what clients in your market expect to pay, and what the rate means for your workload.

Three checks on any rate

  1. Cost floor. The minimum rate from income, costs, taxes and billable hours. Quoting below this means working at a loss.
  2. Market range. What comparable freelancers in your field and region charge. Job boards, professional associations and peer conversations give useful ranges.
  3. Value. What the result is worth to the client. A deliverable that saves a client significant money can justify pricing above the market range.

If the cost floor sits above the market range, the fix is usually more billable hours, lower costs, or a different niche, not a lower rate.

Taxes for U.S. freelancers

Self-employment tax is 15.3% on most net earnings (12.4% Social Security up to the annual wage base, plus 2.9% Medicare), on top of income tax. The IRS generally expects quarterly estimated payments, and you can deduct half of the self-employment tax. Setting aside a fixed share of every payment in a separate account makes the quarterly bills less painful.

Hourly, project or retainer?

  • Hourly suits open-ended work where the scope is likely to change.
  • Project pricing rewards efficiency, but needs a written scope and a limit on revisions.
  • Retainers trade a small discount for predictable monthly income, which can raise your billable share.

Raising your rate

Review your rate at least once a year. Apply new rates to new clients first, and give existing clients advance notice, such as 30 to 60 days, with the new rate and the date it starts.
